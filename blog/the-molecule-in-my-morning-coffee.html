Off The Charts Issue 002 Health. Data. No shortcuts.
Off The Charts Issue 002

The Molecule
in My Morning
Coffee.

A routine blood test, a broken healthcare system, and an AI conversation that found what a doctor couldn't be bothered to look for.

It started in September.

That's when my doctor wrote the requisition for a routine blood panel. Nothing alarming — the standard annual checkup. The kind of thing you're supposed to do and mostly don't.

The requisition got lost somewhere between the clinic and my life. By mid-February I'd finally gotten a replacement copy, and then proceeded to deprioritize it for another two months because, well, life. Work. A new baby at home. The usual. Eventually, sometime in mid-April, I ran out of excuses and went.

Seven months after the original prescription. Classic.

In hindsight, the delay was the least dysfunctional part of this story.

The Phone Call

A few days after the blood draw, my new family doctor called with the results.

I should say upfront: I don't have high confidence in this guy. Nothing catastrophic on the surface — he just doesn't inspire the kind of reassurance you want from someone nominally responsible for your health. The call did nothing to change that.

He went through the results in a way that strongly suggested he was reading them for the first time while talking to me. Everything looks okay, he said. But your LDL seems a bit high. And your vitamin D is insufficient.

On the vitamin D, I couldn't help myself.

"Of course it is," I told him. "We live in Canada. Eight months of winter, no sun. I'm honestly just surprised to be alive."

He moved on.

On the LDL, things deteriorated further. He didn't sound confident. He said — and I want to be precise here because this matters — "let me put your numbers into my software to determine if we need to medicate you."

That sentence. Right there.

Not "let me look at the rest of your panel." Not "let me see how this fits with your other markers." The man looked at one flagged number and his immediate move was to open a decision-tree application and start typing.

The software asked him to ask me things. How much do you weigh? I stared at the phone. We've met. You've seen me. This is in my file. I told him anyway. When did you last check your blood pressure? You took it. Last appointment. I explained we have a machine at home and it reads within what I think is normal.

He concluded that according to the software, medication wasn't required. But I should make some dietary changes and lifestyle adjustments, and we'd recheck in six months.

That was the clinical assessment. In its entirety.

"I have junior engineers whose instinct when looking at anomalous data is to check whether other signals corroborate or contradict the outlier. My doctor's instinct was to open a software and type in a number. One of those approaches is called troubleshooting. The other is called data entry."

And here's what genuinely bothers me: if that software had crossed a threshold and recommended a statin, would he have pushed back? Would he have looked at the broader picture and said "wait, something doesn't add up here"? Based on everything about that phone call — I don't think so. He would have recommended medication to a patient whose actual cardiovascular risk profile, read properly, was quite good. And most patients, trusting their doctor, would have taken it.

I asked him to send me the results. He did. And then I opened Claude.

A Note on the System

Before getting into the actual science, it's worth naming something.

What I'm describing isn't just one mediocre doctor. The Canadian primary care system is structurally designed to produce exactly this interaction. Appointments are short by design — physicians are overwhelmed, underfunded, and operating essentially as triage filters rather than diagnosticians. Algorithmic decision-support tools proliferate precisely because there isn't time (or perhaps knowledge) for actual clinical reasoning.

The system has optimized for volume, not reasoning.

What I found through an hour of conversation with an AI would probably have taken fifteen additional minutes in that phone call. Fifteen minutes of actually looking at the data. That's not a resource problem. That's a priorities problem.

Reading the Panel Properly

Four pages of numbers from Dynacare, faxed at 1:25am on a Saturday. I fed them into the AI and asked for a real assessment — not a checklist, but an actual read of what the data was saying together.

What came back immediately reframed the picture.

The flagged results were two: LDL cholesterol at 3.86 mmol/L against a reference of < 3.50, and vitamin D at 60 nmol/L in the insufficiency band. Everything else — fasting glucose, HbA1c, kidney function, liver enzymes, thyroid, full blood count, urine — was clean. Some of it was genuinely excellent.

The vitamin D I'd already made peace with. Canada. April. Moving on.

The LDL was the interesting one. Because sitting right next to it were numbers that told a contradictory story — and this contradiction is precisely what the phone call never touched.

The Ratios The Doctor Didn't Mention

Here's something that wasn't discussed in my appointment, and probably isn't discussed in most appointments: LDL in isolation is a crude cardiovascular risk metric. What matters more is the relationship between lipid markers — and those relationships carry very different predictive weights.

There are two ratios that cardiologists and lipid researchers increasingly treat as more meaningful than LDL alone. It should be noted that the details around these ratios were actually present on the results provided — just never discussed.

TC/HDL Ratio — Total Cholesterol divided by HDL

This captures the balance between all potentially harmful lipoproteins and the protective HDL that clears them from your arteries. A ratio below 5.0 is considered acceptable risk. Below 4.0 is optimal. Mine was 4.0 — sitting right at the optimal threshold, despite the flagged LDL number that kicked off the whole software consultation.

Triglyceride/HDL Ratio — the one that really seems to matter

This is the marker that wasn't mentioned at all, and arguably should have led the conversation.

The Trig/HDL ratio has emerged in cardiovascular research as one of the strongest predictors of actual heart disease risk — in several major studies, more predictive than LDL alone. The reason is that it functions as a proxy for LDL particle quality, something a standard blood panel doesn't directly measure.

LDL isn't one uniform thing. LDL particles come in different sizes — small, dense particles that oxidize easily and embed in arterial walls, and large, buoyant particles that are far less dangerous even at equivalent concentrations. A standard panel just measures total LDL. It tells you nothing about which type predominates.

The Trig/HDL ratio tells you indirectly. High triglycerides combined with low HDL strongly predicts the dangerous small dense variety. Low triglycerides combined with adequate HDL predicts the relatively benign large buoyant variety.

A Trig/HDL ratio of 0.59 places me in the outstanding category — the top tier of cardiovascular risk profiles by this measure. The same blood panel that produced a flagged LDL also produced a Trig/HDL ratio that most people would be glad to have.

And critically: triglycerides at 0.82 are the direct metabolic fingerprint of someone who is aerobically fit and insulin sensitive. Sedentary people with declining metabolic health typically show elevated triglycerides alongside elevated LDL. Mine were excellent.

So the pattern was specific — one elevated marker, everything else pointing toward a healthy metabolism. Something was specifically driving LDL up without disrupting anything else. Working through the variables one by one, two real contributors emerged.

Variable One: The Molecule in My Morning Coffee

I had never heard of cafestol before this conversation. I'd wager most people haven't. And based on my recent experience, I'm not convinced most primary care physicians think about it either.

Cafestol is a diterpene molecule found naturally in coffee oil — present in all coffee, in the bean itself, nothing to do with quality or processing. Its companion compound is kahweol. They were first isolated by Dutch researchers in the 1950s, but the mechanism connecting them to LDL elevation wasn't understood until the 1990s, through landmark work by Martijn Katan and colleagues at Wageningen University in the Netherlands.

The mechanism is worth understanding properly.

The liver continuously converts cholesterol into bile acids, secreted into the intestine to help digest fat, then reabsorbed and recycled back. The liver monitors bile acid levels through a receptor called FXR and uses that signal to regulate how much cholesterol it synthesizes and how aggressively it clears LDL from the bloodstream.

Cafestol suppresses FXR signaling. The liver perceives a bile acid shortage that doesn't exist, and responds by doing two things simultaneously: ramping up cholesterol synthesis, and reducing LDL receptor activity — meaning less LDL gets cleared from circulation.

"Production up. Clearance down. At the same time. From a molecule in your morning coffee."

The quantified effect, from a meta-analysis pooling multiple controlled trials: approximately 0.13 mmol/L of LDL per cup per day of unfiltered coffee.

I drink at least three cups per day. French press, moka pot. Good beans, careful technique, a ritual I take seriously.

Three cups. 0.13 per cup. Potentially 0.39 mmol/L of my LDL result directly attributable to my brewing method — against a threshold gap of 0.36 mmol/L.

The molecule in my morning coffee may have been my entire out-of-range result.

How filtering comes into play

In the late 1980s, Norwegian researchers noticed that Norwegians had significantly higher cholesterol levels than comparable Scandinavian populations, and nobody could explain it through diet. They eventually traced it to coffee preparation — Norwegians predominantly used boiled, steeped methods while neighboring populations used filtered drip more commonly.

Controlled trials followed. Same coffee, same people, same quantity — different preparation method, measurable LDL difference.

The mechanism is purely physical. Cafestol is a large molecule that gets trapped in paper filters. It passes freely through metal mesh — french press, moka pot — because the pores are too large. Paper catches it before it reaches your cup. Espresso sits in an interesting middle position: the compressed puck of grounds and short contact time results in significantly less cafestol extraction than immersion methods.

The obvious recommendation was to switch to filtered drip. I find filtered drip genuinely undrinkable. That's not a swap, that's a punishment.

The real answer: switch to espresso as my primary method. Which I already love, have been drinking for years, and requires zero sacrifice. Same ritual, same quantity, a fraction of the cafestol.

That's what targeted diagnosis does. The intervention doesn't have to be dramatic. It just has to be specific.

Variable Two: Sleep

This one I can't currently fix. There's a newborn in the house.

But it belongs in the honest accounting. Poor and fragmented sleep elevates cortisol chronically — and cortisol directly stimulates hepatic cholesterol synthesis through the same liver pathway that cafestol disrupts. Research on sleep and lipids consistently shows that sleeping less than six hours is associated with measurably higher LDL and lower HDL.

You can't optimize your way around a newborn. But naming it matters — it means some portion of my current LDL elevation is transient and situational, not structural. It will resolve on its own, eventually, when the guy responsible for 3am wake-ups figures out that everyone prefers sleeping.

What This Is Really About

I want to be precise about the conclusion, because it's easy to reach for the wrong one.

This isn't really a story about AI. It's a story about what happens when someone actually looks at data.

My doctor had the same four pages I did. He had medical training I don't have. He had access to reference ranges, clinical guidelines, years of patient experience. What he apparently didn't have — or didn't use — was the inclination to look at multiple data points and ask whether they told a coherent story together.

The Trig/HDL ratio of 0.59 was right there. The contradiction between an excellent metabolic profile and a single elevated marker was right there. The question of why LDL would be elevated in someone who swims 7.5 kilometres a week and has outstanding triglycerides — that question was right there, waiting to be asked.

It wasn't asked. A number was entered into software. A recommendation was generated. The call ended.

I'm not naive about what AI is and isn't. It's a reasoning tool. It surfaces frameworks, mechanisms, and research that would take hours to compile manually. It asks good sequential questions. It doesn't have an agenda, doesn't have twelve other patients waiting, and doesn't have a billing code to hit.

But the actual thinking — knowing my own habits, correcting wrong assumptions as they surfaced, understanding my own history and context — that was mine. The AI gave me the instrument. I ran the investigation.

"A molecule called cafestol, sitting in the oils of my french press coffee, was almost certainly responsible for pushing one marker above a threshold — a marker that, in context, pointed toward no meaningful cardiovascular concern at all. The software would have medicated that."

My next bloodwork is in six months. We'll see if the math holds.
